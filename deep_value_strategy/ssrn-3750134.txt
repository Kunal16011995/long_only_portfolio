Deep Value Portfolios : a Long Only Implementation
Philippe Declerck* This version: December 2020
Abstract We describe a framework to build deep value multi-asset long-only portfolios. Portfolios built according to this framework do add value, and are robust to several changes of specifications. Much like traditional value portfolios, we document they work better when combined with some sort of momentum signal. Keywords: deep value

1 Introduction
Value is the phenomenon that cheap securities will be to prone to outperform in the near future, while expensive securities will be to prone to underperform in the near future. It is a relative phenomenon: a portfolio investing according to value will be long of the cheap assets and short of the expensive ones. Hence, even if cheap securities display negative absolute returns in the near future, the value portfolio may deliver positive return, provided cheap securites outperform expensive ones.
It is hard to identify the first value strategy (or investor), but what is certain is that its principle has been known and applied for quite some time, with for instance Graham and Dodd (1934, [15]). The value investing literature is large and ever expanding, with now well-known work published in the 1980s and 1990s, for instance DeBondt and Thaler (1985, [11]), Fama and French (1992, [13] and 1993, [14]), Lakonishok, Shleifer, and Vishny (1994, [17]) and many others.
Value is not a simply a beta phonemenon, and literature supports the idea that partial implementation of factors, through the long leg only, adds material value. Asness et al. (2012, [2]) show that the long leg of value portfolios delivers superior risk-adjusted returns compared to the short leg in many markets and asset classes. Blitz et al. (2020, [9]) go as far as to say that investors will be better off with simplified portfolios using the long leg and shorting with the market, since "the long-minus-market approach has offered more value than the full-fledged long-short approach for individual factors, and even more so for a multi-factor combination". Benaych-Georges et al. (2020, [7]) revisit this paper, and while their conclusion differs on the effectiveness of the short leg, one of their findings is that the "LH [long leg beta-hedged] implementation of individual factors leads to Sharpe Ratios that are comparable, on average, to those of the LS [long-short] implementation".
While the term "deep value" sounds familiar, there is not a single, well-accepted defintion. Intuitively, deep value investing demands an incredibly high discount relative to "fair value". Things would be simple if the concept of fair value was well defined, but it is not. If fair value is an anchor, it could be an assessment of the value of a given security, using, for instance for equities, (tangible) book value, discounted cash flows or dividends,. . . , or a historical value, around which the market value gravitates. Asness et al. (2017, [1]) define deep value "as episodes where the valuation spread between cheap and expensive securities is wide relative to its history". This definition implies a comparison between 2 assets and from a historical basis. According to Sharma et al. (2019, [19]) "deep value investors look for the largest dislocations and hold concentrated portfolios of stocks with the cheapest valuations relative to intrinsic value". This view calls for both
2 Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=3750134

historical and cross-sectional comparisons. Hence, deep value can be assessed from a historical and / or a cross-sectional view, and also on a standalone or relative basis. For instance Asness et al. (2017, [1]) consider assets on a historical and relative basis, while typical value screens of benchmarked equities long-only value strategies tend consider stocks on a cross-sectional (within sectors or industries) and relative basis.
In this paper, we opt to focus on a historical definition of deep value, and consider assets on a standalone basis. Our goal is to build long-only portfolios that are easy to implement in real life, because investing in the simplest relative value portfolio (dollar neutral, long one asset, short one asset) is not always achievable, because of potential (net) short constraints. Therefore, we build deep value portfolios that are fully implementable by investing out-ofsample in securities identified as deep value by the metric used (more on this in Sections 3 and 4).
We contribute to the scarce literature on deep value by systematically building investable, out-of-sample, portfolios based on extreme realizations of a metric derived from a value framework. We also test the robustness of the framework, and our empirical results show that it is robust to variations of parameters, and that complementing deep value with a momentum overlay does add value.
The rest of the paper is organised as follows. Section 2 provides an overview of the dataset. Section 3 describes the framework used to build the expected returns from which signals are used. These signals and the subsequent multi-asset portfolios are described in Section 4. Section 5 presents a first set of results, while Section 6 expands them and reports robustness checks, and finally Section 7 concludes.

This framework heavily relies on market prices and fundamental dynamics, depends on a limited set of parameters only, and do not incorporate many judgemental views (except the model itself, which is no insignificant view), except for some prospective views from consensus. Series derived from this framework can be updated, monitored and used on a real-time basis in financial simulations and backtests.

4 Portfolio Construction
4.1 Signals
The valuation metric is determined over the asset classes and securities mentioned in Section 2 and derived from the expected return defined in Section 3. Rather than using the expected returns outright, we use rolling 10y percentiles of the expected returns. High (resp. low) percentiles are equivalent to high (resp. low) expected returns hence equivalent to low (resp. high) valuations. Therefore, an asset will be flagged as "Deep Value" (i.e. extremely cheap) when its percentile is very high.
Rolling 10y percentiles are calculated for each asset in all asset classes. When the available history ranges between 5 and 10 years, percentiles are calculated over the available history. No percentile is calculated when less than 5 years of data are available.

4.2 Portfolios
The typical value investor buys and sells early. The obvious risk of a deep value investor is to buy a value trap, i.e. to buy extremely early. According to Sharma et al. (2019, [19]), "the risk of value traps - that low prices may remain low for a long time - is exacerbated with deep value strategies". Kupersmith et al. (2017, [16]) advise to combine "quantitative and qualitative insights" when evaluating deep value opportunities, or to "incorporate other systematic investment themes, like quality".
To benefit from the re-rating of deep value assets, we need to avoid value traps while retaining deep value assets until the re-rating is material enough. Hence, we need to define the following thresholds:
· IN: the entry threshold ;
· OUT: the natural exit threshold, when the asset has re-rated enough ;
· ValueTrap: the alternative exit threshold, in case the asset keeps getting cheaper.
An asset is considered deep value when the corresponding historical percentile P is such as: P  IN and P < V alueT rap. At time t, an asset will be in portfolio if:
· it is flagged as deep value: the corresponding percentile at t Pt is such as Pt  IN and Pt < V alueT rap
· it was in portfolio at time t - 1 and Pt is such as Pt > OU T and Pt < V alueT rap.
Limiting value traps means ValueTrap should not be much larger than OUT: if the asset is considered cheap enough around IN, we should not allow it to become much cheaper before exiting. Using a ValueTrap threshold prevents from investing in a security that keeps derating. It can be seen as a momentum filter: while we're not looking for positive momentum per se, we are leaving aside securities with negative momentum. Riding a deep value asset long enough means OUT should be much smaller than IN, so we need to have: OU T < IN < V alueT rap and OU T IN . Figure 2 illustrates this rationale.
Portfolios are built at the asset class level. For a given asset class with N assets, assets are allocated weights of 1/N . To ensure no look-ahead bias, the signals used at date t are

6 Robutsness checks
The first part of our empirical analysis essentially describes our modus oprandi, and only displays results for a single deep value strategy, derived from one set of parameters. In this section we test the robustness of strategies to all three parameters.
6.1 The IN, OUT and ValueTrap thresholds
Table 5 displays Sharpe ratios for a first set of alternative strategies: we keep OUT at 50%, while move IN between 80% and 90%, with ValueTrap remaining between 5% and 15% higher than IN. Table 6 displays the corresponding ranks of these Sharpe ratios. The first observation is that widening the gap between IN and ValueTrap tends to deteriorate Sharpe ratios, except for DM Equities, which seem insensitive to these thresholds. This makes sense: the wider the gap between IN and ValueTrap, the more space for valuation deterioration. The gap between IN and ValueTrap needs to be kept narrow. The second observation is that all strategies rank very well against random strategies, except for EM Equities. Two of the tested alternatives fail to outperform half of the random strategies.
The second set of alternative strategies test the impact of the OUT threshold, while keeping IN at 80% and ValueTrap at 90%. Table 7 shows three different behvaviors. For Bonds and EMEquities, the Sharpe ratio is going one way: up, while it is going down for DMEquities. For DMFX, the Sharpe ratio starts by increasing, then decreases (much more), while it seems to oscillate for EMFX. These differences are understandable: they are a sign that trends have been much more long-lasting in Bonds over the past 20 years. Table 8 reports that most strategies have massively outperformed random strategies. Using 70% for the OUT threshold gives weak results for all asset classes, and from a relative view, the only strategies underperfoming are the ones with a 70% OUT threshold. This confirms the intuition that the gap between IN and OUT should not be too small.
The third set of alternative strategies also test the impact of the OUT threshold, this time keeping IN at 90% and ValueTrap at 95%. Tables 9 and 10 confirm previous results, with slight differences. Indeed, when OUT=70%, only EM Equities are badly ranked, and while Sharpe ratios of Bonds and FX strategies are higher on average, Sharpe ratios of

Equities strategies are slightly lower on average. With respect to Bonds and EM Equities, it has been better to hold on to deep value assets for long, while with respect to FX and EM Equities, it has better not to hold on too long.
6.2 Does trying to avoid value traps help ?
We may legitimately wonder whether adding a ValueTrap threshold, a kind of momentum overlay, adds value in a deep value framework. Therefore, we ran the same tests as in 6.1, but without a ValueTrap threshold. Tables 11 and 12 display results when the IN threshold varies, while Tables 13, 14, 15 and 16 display results with IN set at 80% and 90%, and OUT moving from 70% to 20%.
Overall, not using the ValueTrap threshold has a material impact on deep value strategies: Sharpe ratios drop significantly, usually by 30% to 60%. That can be even more for DM FX, for which Sharpe ratios become overall negative, or less for EM Equities, for which not using ValueTrap even increase the Sharpe ratio in a few instances (though by only a few basis points). When compared to random strategies, these deep value strategies do not fare well on average, and fail to beat half of random strategies more often than not. Lone exceptions are DM Equities, and EM FX to a lesser extent.
7 Conclusion
Building expected returns and deriving deep value strategies from them has worked over the past 20 years. Indeed, deep value strategies tend to deliver positive Sharpe ratios and outperform the luckiest random strategies (in terms of Sharpe ratios). These deep value strategies are not often invested, hence can be used as overlays. They have performed well in different asset classes, and they seem robust to the performance pattern of underlying assets and thresholds used. Interestingly, they also tend to perform better when they are completed with a momentum filter. The obvious caveat is that history length is only 20 years, and it will be worth expanding it in future research. Also, one has to keep in mind that, by definition, percentile analysis is not forward looking, and that things can always get (a lot) worse before they eventually get better.
From an practical view, portfolios were designed according to 3 thresholds: IN, OUT and ValueTrap. Robustness checks show deep value portfolios are robust to these thresholds, and help draw rules: OUT should be low enough compared to IN, though to too low, because the added value of keeping assets for a very long time appears limited.
