Implementing a Systematic Long-only Momentum Strategy:
Evidence From India
Rajan Raju Abhijit Chandrasekaran
October 2019
ABSTRACT
We show that a monthly-rebalanced, long-only portfolio of top-decile stocks selected from the
NIFTY100 using ‘off-the-shelf’ momentum criteria significantly outperforms the NIFTY100 Index -
both in terms of absolute returns (by 10.70% pa) and risk adjusted returns, with a mean turnover of
32.10% per month. We show that momentum persists in the near term but dissipates over time. We
demonstrate that our long-only approach has a significant tilt to the momentum factor. We also show
that time in the market rather than timing the market is important for momentum investing. The
strategy has higher volatility and the occasional momentum crash. The strategy’s out performance
survives real-world implementation given the rise of discount brokers in India. In the absence of cheap
ETFs to get exposure to momentum, the systematic long-only strategy from the most liquid part of
the market using ‘off-the-shelf’ criteria provides a practical, executable investment methodology that
exposes an investor to momentum in the Indian market.

1 Introduction and Literature Review
“far more money is made buying high and selling at even higher prices” - Richard Driehaus
Factor investing is a rational and robust investment approach for long term investors. Factors
are investment styles that deliver outsize returns over the long run. Stocks have exposure to style
factors like value, momentum, and quality. However, factors are not a one way street - they are risky
and can under-perform1. Equities and bonds are instances of factors where risk premiums are col-
lected by simply buying them and are referred to as static factors. Most factor strategies follow Fama
and French’s (1993) seminal work on factors and build long-short market-neutral portfolios based on
a universe of all securities in the relevant market. These dynamic factor portfolios are rebalanced
regularly. While factor theory is robust, transforming a non-investable risk factor into an investable
strategy is complex. Two strategies based on the same factor may behave differently depending upon
the choice of implementation (asset universe, choice of metric, cut-off threshold, weighting scheme,
rebalancing frequency)2. We specifically flag two issues. First, short selling is restricted and often
prohibited for retail investors. Alternatives to short selling are expensive and have execution risks not
accounted for in theory. Long-only portfolios are unbalanced portfolios carrying market exposures as
distinct from market-neutral long-short portfolios. Second, market liquidity/capacity is an execution
constraint that is difficult to model accurately and practical implementations usually deliver different
(and often lower) returns than what theory suggests.
In their original work on buying past winners and selling past losers, Jegadeesh and Titman (1993)
laid the foundations of the current momentum framework. They showed picking recent winners from
the past 3-12 months and holding them for the next 3-12 months provides abnormal returns. Systemic
risks could not explain these returns. Beyond a 12-month holding period, they showed the perfor-
mance of the winners dissipates over the next 24 months.
SinceJegadeeshandTitman’swork,severalstudiesshowthatthemomentumanomalyholdsacross
markets, asset classes and regimes. Rouwenhorst (1998) shows significant momentum returns from
a sample of 12 European countries and a sample of 20 emerging markets (1999). In a study of 23
countries across Asia-Pacific, Europe, Japan, and North America, Fama and French (2012) report
‘except for Japan, there is return momentum everywhere, and spreads in average momentum returns
also decrease from smaller to bigger stocks’. Asness, Moskowitz, and Pedersen (2013) find evidence
for consistent momentum return premia across eight diverse markets and asset classes.
In India, too, momentum studies show evidence of momentum premium. Ansari and Khan (2012)
find ‘strong presence’ of momentum profits in Indian stock between 1995-2006. Maheshwari and
Dhankar (2018) using data between 1997-2013 ‘confirm the existence of momentum and long-term
overreaction effect in the Indian stock market’.
Any momentum anomaly is difficult to reconcile with the efficient market hypothesis. If the stock
pricefullyreflectsallavailableinformation, thereisnologicalexplanationastowhystockswhichhave
done well (poorly) in the recent past will continue to do well (poorly) in the ‘near term’. Explanations
for the anomaly can be categorised into two broad themes:
• Economic: the higher returns compensate the additional risk investors take when they invest
in a momentum strategy (see Li, Miffre, Brooks, and O’Sullivan, 2008).

• Behavioural: the price under- and over-reaction results from the cognitive errors investors

areslowtoadjusttheirinvestmentviewswhenpresentedwithnew(andespeciallycontradictory)
information. Or investors use the representativeness heuristic - they are too quick to conclude
that a stock follows a particular ‘ideal type’. Citing evidence that over 3- to 5- year holding
periods, stocks which performed poorly over the previous 3 to 5 years had higher returns than
stocks that performed well over the same period, Bondt and Thaler (1985, 1987) argued that
investorstendtooverreacttoinformation. Daniel,Hirshleifer,andSubrahmanyam(1998)builda
behaviouralmodelbasedontheoverconfidenceofindividualstoexplainthemomentumanomaly.
Barberis, Shleifer, and Vishny (1998) propose a model that is consistent with the empirical
findings of pervasive under reaction of stock prices to news such as earnings announcements,
and over reaction of stock prices to a series of good (bad) news. The behavioural attributes
create momentum as stock prices react with delay to firm-specific information and subsequent
reversion once deviations from equilibrium are acknowledged.
Despite widespread empirical evidence3 for the momentum anomaly and some strong theoretical
hypothesis to explain it, there remain serious debates around momentum investing. The most sig-
nificant is whether the out-performance of such portfolios survive transaction costs in the real-world.
The intuitive economic argument is simple. Investors will demand more of the stocks selected by the
strategycreatingupwardpricepressurethatwill,inevitably,lowerexpectedreturns. Conversely,when
investors exit existing positions, their selling lowers prices, reducing realized return. Unfortunately,
these dynamics of demand and supply on capacity and costs are not easily observable.
A number of studies have estimated capacity for factor-strategies (see Zhiwu Chen (2002), An-
drea Frazzini (2012), Novy-Marx and Velikov (2014) and Ratcliffe et al. (2017)). Vogel (2017) surveys
literature on both sides of the survival of momentum returns in the real world and concludes that the
answer to the cost/capacity question depends on the assumptions and data used. Feifei, Tzee-Man,
Pickard, and Garg (2019) build a model for the market impact of such costs for a number of factor-
based strategies, including momentum. Israel, Ross, Moskowitz, and Serban (2017b) use the live track
record of the implementation of momentum by AQR and ‘show that momentum is an implementable
strategy and that the live experience of trading has not necessarily produced excessive turnover, large
trading costs, or significant tax burdens. These results contradict common myths about momentum
investing’. Frazzini, Israel, and Moskowitz (2018) using data from actual trading costs from a large
institutional asset manager argue that actual costs from live trades are significantly lower than what
academic studies suggest.
Despite the ongoing debate on the survival of momentum returns in the real-world, there is unan-
imous consensus that excessive transaction costs are a prime destroyer of excess returns. Novy-Marx
andVelikov(2014)showthatmostofthefactorswithone-sidedmonthlyturnoverhigherthan50%are
not profitable after trading costs. Transaction costs reduce the factor profitability and its associated
statistical significance. For an investor, transaction costs are primarily brokerage, custody and taxes.
Other than taxes, costs are being driven down by the increasing use of fintech, digitisation and digital-
isation. Portfolio turnover remains an important determinant of whether a practical implementation
is profitable.
Unfortunately, similar studies on momentum cost/capacity do not exist for the Indian market.
Kumar and Misra (2019) find ‘liquidity forms part of the systematic and idiosyncratic risk. Therefore,
failure to incorporate it into portfolio formulation strategies may lead investors on NSE to take er-

A word about long-short portfolios. These portfolios are long the preferred exposure and short
the unwanted exposure in equal measure. For exposure to the momentum factor, the portfolio would
buy stocks exhibiting strong positive momentum and sell stocks with strong negative momentum. In
reality, it is far easier to buy stocks than it is to sell stocks that one does not own. The short leg is
challenging at best and often impossible to execute. It should be no surprise that many of the factor
ETFs listed in the US are actually long-only portfolios that are tilted toward or away from particular
factor exposures. Long-only factor portfolios will not fully replicate the performance of long-short
factors. Huij, Lansdorp, Blitz, and van Vliet (2014) examine the differences between long-only and
long-short portfolios and find ‘that although a long-short approach is superior theoretically, a long-only
approach seems to be the preferred alternative in most scenarios, after accounting for practical issues
such as benchmark restrictions, implementation costs and factor decay’4.
Accessing factors cheaply is impossible for most non-institutional investors in India. There are no
ETFs offering specific factor exposure and neither do mutual funds give such targeted exposure. For a
self-directedinvestor, anyDo-It-Yourselffactor-exposureportfoliowouldneedtobeeasytoadminister
and cheap to execute. With the growth of discount brokers, transaction costs, like brokerage, have
dropped significantly for all investors. Structured data is increasingly accessible at a reasonable cost.
Retail investors can easily access and use data from financial reports and price data from exchanges
to build and manage their investment portfolios.
Given the constraints of short selling in India, the lack low-cost instruments that target factors,
the low cost of brokerage and the increasingly easy access to structured data it seems appropriate,
therefore, to examine the possibility of building a systematic long-only momentum portfolio. We look
to build an easily understood and practically implementable strategy using ‘off-the-academic-shelf’
measures. Our objective is to robustly examine the practical implementation of such a strategy. We
recognize that there can be different implementation choices leading to different outcomes. We do not
seek to optimize a strategy using data analysis and falling prey to data snooping5 and/or over-fitting.
This paper attempts to translate factor theory into practice for the general self-directed investor
and extends current thinking in at least three areas. First, geographically, we believe it is one of the
early in-depth exploration of build practical momentum strategies within the Indian context. Second,
quantitative, systematic strategies are still largely of interest amongst institutional investors. This
paper is one of the early efforts to extend factor theory to non-institutional investors. We believe
both areas require more research and debate to raise the standards of discussion amongst and choices
of investment for the wider investment community. Third, the paper makes an essential distinction
between the signal for portfolio construction and the execution price for rebalancing. Traditionally,
most studies have not made this distinction, and we are one of the early papers to explore this aspect
within an Indian context.

All data is from Refinitiv. Price data is daily close prices for the purposes of analysis and portfolio

Our universe is the constituents stocks of the NIFTY100 Index (NIFTY100) during the period

3.4 Overnight Risk - does our execution strategy make a difference to expected
returns
Traditionally, the calculation of factors ignores the fact that all stock exchanges allow trading only for
a limited number of hours and generally choose closing prices for the calculations. This has immediate
real world issues : an investor cannot use the closing prices to analyse the universe and simultane-
ously make the investment. Closing prices are the last traded price before the market closes, and the
probability of being the last trade is very low. While the skip-month deals with the signal and execu-
tion challenge, we believe that a more pragmatic option is to use the closing prices for the portfolio
construction, and use open prices for execution24.
Using closing prices for portfolio construction and opening prices for execution raises the issue of
‘overnight’ risk : open prices are not the same as the closing prices of the previous trading day. With
many corporate announcements done after the stock exchange is closed and an increasing number of
firms with businesses that are transnational, the ‘overnight period’25 is a source of price jump risk.
At the open of a new trading day, the ‘overnight’ information is reflected in the opening price for the
stock. Oldfield and Rogalski (1980) developed the hypothesis that the return distributions for trading
andnon-tradingperiodswereindeeddistinct. HongandWang(2000)buildamodelthatdemonstrates
that ‘open-to-open returns are more volatile than close-to-close returns and intraday trading volume is
U-shaped’ reflecting empirical evidence. Liu and Tse (2016) show ‘overnight returns of US exchange-
traded index funds and most international index futures are significantly positive, while returns during
trading hours are negative’. Ahoniemi and Lanne (2013) examine a number of solutions to deal with
the volatility of overnight returns.
Open prices are usually determined by the pre-open session where an ‘equilibrium’ price becomes
theofficialopenprice. Intheeventthatthereisnopricediscoveryinthepre-opensession,thepricefor
the first trade in the normal market serves as the opening price. For our analysis, we will use the data
from the NIFTY100 index rather than averaging the prices of individual stocks. We do this to illus-
tratetheprincipleandoutlinetheframework for analysis. Ananalysisforindividualstockstoidentify
potential stock-specific idiosyncrasies may need to be incorporated in a more detailed implementation.
The NIFTY100 closing prices are calculated by taking the last half an hour weighted average clos-
ing prices of the constituents of the index. At open, NIFTY100 opening prices are calculated from
the opening prices of the constituents of the index. It is possible, therefore, for stale prices to be part
of the calculation of both the open and close prices for the index.

the period of time under analysis. This gives an indication of the range of outcomes for an investor
across a period of time. Rolling returns provide a robust analytical tool for evaluating performance.
This is especially important during volatile periods when entering a strategy a few months forward or
backward from any given date can deliver vastly different returns. We show rolling returns in Figure
