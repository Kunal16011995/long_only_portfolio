Implementing a Systematic Long-only Quality Strategy in the Hong
Kong Market
Rajan Raju
November 2019
ABSTRACT
We believe investors should be willing to pay a higher price for higher quality companies. We
build a composite quality score, including one which incorporates publicly available ESG scores, us-
ing ‘off-the-shelf’ criteria and publicly available financial data and show that a quarterly-rebalanced,
long-onlyportfoliooftop-decilestocksselectedusingourscoreinHongKongsignificantlyoutperforms
the Hang Seng Composite LargeCap Index (HSLI) - both in terms of absolute returns (by 2.87% pa)
and risk adjusted returns - while having an moderate annual turnover (a mean turnover of 42.15%).
We show that our quality score predicts the persistence of quality for up to 3 years and there is a
weak relationship between the price multiple and the quality score. Furthermore, we demonstrate
that quality needs to be reviewed regularly - and the moderate turnover ensures this. In the absence
of cheap ETFs to get systematic exposure to quality, the systematic long-only strategy using ‘off-the-
shelf’ criteria provides a practical, executable systematic investment methodology that exposes an
investor to quality in the Hong Kong market. We show that socially conscious investors can generate
risk adjusted returns in excess of the HSLI in the Hong Kong market using a systematic strategy.

1 Introduction and Literature Review
“It’s far better to buy a wonderful company at a fair price than a fair company at a wonderful
price.” - Warren Buffet
We recognize that factor investing is a rational and robust investment methodology for long term
investors. Factors are investment styles that deliver outsize returns over the long run. Stocks have
exposure to style factors like value, momentum, and quality. However, factors are not a one way
street - they do have risk, can under-perform and suffer from drawdowns in the ‘short run’1. Equities
and bonds are instances of factors whose risk premiums are obtained by simply buying them (long-
only positions), hence referred to as ‘static factors’. Most factor strategies follow Fama and French’s
(1993) seminal work on factors and build long-short market-neutral portfolios based on a universe of
all securities in the relevant market irrespective of market liquidity. These are ‘dynamic’ factor port-
folios as they are rebalanced regularly. While factor theory is robust, the practical implementation
immediately runs into real world problems. Specifically, we flag two. First, short selling is restricted
andoftennotpermittedforretailinvestors. Alternativemechanismstoshortsellingareexpensiveand
come with execution risks that are not accounted for in factor theory. The counter-point to not being
able to go short is that a long-only portfolio is an ‘unbalanced’ portfolio and has market exposures as
distinct from a market-neutral portfolio. Second, market liquidity is a real constraint and therefore
practicalimplementationsoftendeliverdifferent(andoftenlower)returnsthanwhatthetheorywould
suggest.
Accessing factors cheaply is nigh impossible for most non-institutional investors in Hong Kong as
there are no ETFs which offer such specific factor exposure, and mutual funds are not tailored specif-
ically for such targeted exposure. For a self-directed investor, any Do-It-Yourself factor-exposure
portfolio would need to be easy to administer, have reasonable turnover rates and take advantage of
the low/no cost brokerages that are a result of the growth of FinTech in the financial industry. Added
to this, with the declining cost and increasing access of structured financial information, we believe
that investors will be able to access and use data from financial reports and price data from exchanges
to build and manage their investment portfolios.
It seems appropriate, therefore, to examine if a systematic long-only portfolio constructed using
the same methodology used by academia to evaluate formal factors and using publicly available data
intheHongKongmarketwillgeneratelongtermriskadjustedreturnsinexcessofamarketportfolio2.
For our purposes, we will choose a universe of large stocks listed in Hong Kong intentionally thereby
reducing governance and fair-disclosure related risk3.

Qualityhasitsrootsinfundamentalanalysis(Graham,2006)buthasbecomepopularasasystem-
atic investment style in recent years. Quality as a factor refers to the tendency of high-quality stocks
(typically defined as having more stable earnings, stronger balance sheets and higher margins) to out-
perform low-quality stocks, over a long time horizon. This tendency is well-documented in financial
literature although the actual measures to define ‘quality’ vary widely. Of all the factors, quality is
perhaps the hardest to define as there is no consensus on the best way to measure it. Company’s
earnings, debt levels, cash flows and other measures have all been shown to have explanatory power in

a comprehensive quality measure called Quality Minus Junk (QMJ) and show that the QMJ premium
is large and persistent globally.
There is also enough academic research to show the challenges with quality as a factor for us to
retain a healthy scepticism on using it blindly. For instance, Kalesnik and Kose (2014) argue “quality
is not a factor that reliably commands a premium in its own right. Nonetheless, value investing con-
ditioned upon certain indicators of company quality is a promising strategy”. So we need to have a
robust assessment of the predictive power of our quality score and any alpha generated by a portfolio
built using our quality score.

our quality measure. Section 4 analyses the capability of our quality measure to predict persistence

rebalanced systematic portfolio (Section 4.3). We show that such a portfolio delivers better long term

Having provided the theoretical underpinning for the choice of components, we next build out the
components themselves. First, theory suggests that profitability should be measured as the ‘sustain-
able’ part of profits adjusted for accruals with relation to book value. We average several measures
of profitability to reduce inherent noise (hopefully leaving the sustainable part of profitability intact).
Second, theory suggests that growth should be the ‘increase in sustainable profits in relation to book
values’. We use a 3 year window where possible, and average multiple measures to reduce noise in
teasing out a measure of sustainable growth8. Finally, safety is computed from volatility, beta and
other risk measures. Similar to the other two measures, we average multiple measures to build a
composite measure for safety9. We standardise and transform all scores as described in Appendix A
to allow for different scores to be combined without any inherent statistical bias.

4 Analysis
4.1 Persistence of Quality
We start by examining whether a stock’s quality is persistent i.e. by selecting stocks that were
profitable, growing, and safe in the recent past, are we successfully selecting stocks that display these
characteristics in the future? This is an important first step in determining whether our criteria work.
In an efficient market, one would expect that the returns on quality to be unpredictable.
To examine the predictability of quality, we build two portfolios every quarter: a Top portfolio
consisting of constituent stocks with the highest quality score, and a Bottom portfolio consisting of
constituent stocks with the lowest quality scores for the period. We then compute a mean quality
score across the equal weighted stocks in each portfolio. We compute this average quality score at the
time of the portfolio formation (time t) and in the subsequent quarter and 1, 2 and 3 years (t + 1,
t + 4, t + 8 and t + 12 quarters). We look at the persistence in the subsequent quarter to examine
whether quality is indeed persistent during our portfolio holding period.
Quality scores, by construction, vary monotonically between the two portfolios at the time of
portfolio formation. We use quality scores in the future as a proxy for persistence. For quality to
be persistent, companies with high quality scores today, should maintain high scores in the future.
Table 1 shows that, on average, and subject to survival of a stock, high-quality firms today remain
high-quality firms a quarter and 1, 2 and 3 years into the future. We can statistically reject the null
hypothesis of no difference in each of quality characteristics between today’s classification of high and
low score companies for at least 3 years in the future.
In Table 1, for our holding period of a quarter, the persistence is clear and significant. Over the
longer period, up to 12 quarters, too, there evidence of persistence across all our components and the
aggregatescore. TheProfitabilitycomponentshowstheweakestpersistenceespeciallytwoyearsout17.
Over time, there is a narrowing of the separation between today’s high quality and low quality
companies. This, importantly, reflects the reality that high quality companies do not necessarily stay

To summarize, our results are consistent with the hypothesis that high-quality firms com-
mand higher (scaled) prices. However, the explanatory power of quality is limited, leaving a
large amount of variation in prices unexplained. Our results appear robust to specification
and not driven by effects related to small stocks or by a particular industry or geography.
The multi-dimensional nature of quality makes it difficult to provide a unified explanation for the
quality premium. Critics of quality often argue that quality is not a single systematic risk factor - but
rather a mix of a variety of underlying factors and behavioral biases. But despite these objections,
there is reasonable evidence to show that high quality firms do command higher multiples.

5 Conclusion
We define a quality stock as one that has characteristics that should command, ceterius paribus, a
higher relative price. Deriving from the Gordon Growth Model, we build a quality score using ‘off-the-
shelf’ criteria for profitability, growth and safety (Quality ) based on existing well-regarded research.
3
To this, we extend the definition of quality to include ESG criteria (Quality ). Using fundamental
4
accounting data and price time series, we use these criteria to build a top decile stock, long-only,
quarterly-rebalanced portfolio from the constituents of the HSLI Index. We first show that quality
is persistent over time. Our analysis shows the predictability of the persistence of high-quality for
firms three years into the future. Next, we show that such a portfolio earns higher than market risk
adjusted returns. Our results are consistent with existing studies on the quality factor : quality stocks
have a tendency to be underpriced and junk stocks overpriced by the market.
When we extend our analysis to include ESG criteria, the results remain largely similar to the
Quality definition. This has practical implications for a socially-minded investor within a Hong
3
Kong context.
Our results further indicate that a long-only systematic Quality and Quality strategy handily
3 4
outperforms the HSLI Index which has ETFs accessible by all investors. By restricting the portfolio
to the top decile (10-11) stocks and quarterly rebalancing an individual investor, we reckon, would
administratively have a fairly light touch in managing such a portfolio. In addition, we show that for
such a strategy, the turnover is modest which further makes portfolio management fairly straightfor-
ward. We finally show that the volatility of the portfolio compares favourably to the market volatility
despite the more concentrated portfolio.
By including ESG criteria, we show that a socially-conscious investor can invest in a practical,
systematic quality strategy which outperforms the broad large cap Index.
Our results argue that quality companies need to be regularly reviewed and the quarterly rebal-
ancing inherent in the construction and the moderate turnover resulting ensures this. The strategy,

Appendix A Standardizing and Transforming into a z-score
In order to put each measure on equal footing and combine them in a statistically valid manner, each
month we standardize and transform each variable into a z-score with similar distribution characteris-
tics. In the construction of z-scores, we deal with outliers through winsorization and clipping similar
to the approach used by Standard & Poor’s in their index construction (S&P).
• Z-Scores: We compute z-scores using:
z = (x −µ )/σ (A.1)

• Winsorization: Outlier z-scores are winsorized to ensure that the overall scores are less dis-
torted by extreme values. To do this, for a given average z-score, the values for all securities
are first ranked in ascending order. Then, for scores that lie above 3 or below -3, their value is
clipped to 3 or -3, whichever is applicable.
• Transform: We use standard scalers to scale and transform the winsorized and clipped z-score
series to create a unit ‘normal’ distribution. This allows for the addition of z-scores without
introducing bias of scale.
